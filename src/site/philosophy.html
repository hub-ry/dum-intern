Philosophy

Getting a result from a model and understanding it are different things. Study an idea, try it, then explain it to Dum. Your explanation guides the creation; the underlying model already knows programming.

Learn, then teach

Explaining something forces you to find the edges of what you know. When you have to tell Dum why a loop with an empty list runs zero times, or why five counts as "at least five", you either know it or you find out that you don't. That gap is the point. The lesson and the exercise come first so you have something real to say.

Dum's lessons are small on purpose. One idea at a time is easier to explain, easier to check, and easier to come back to.

Corrections stay visible

Dum doesn't tell you that you've mastered anything. It shows you what it built from your words, whether verification passed, and when a corrected version was needed, both versions sit side by side. You read the result and judge for yourself. A creation that needed a correction is still a useful record, often more useful than one that went right the first time.

Small claims

The workshop covers a few ideas well instead of claiming to cover everything. Verification runs the creation in an isolated container, which is a real check but not a guarantee. The example on this site comes from sample teaching, not from a learner's progress. We'd rather describe exactly what Dum does than promise what it might do.
