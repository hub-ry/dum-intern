Philosophy

Someone on r/cscareerquestions asked: “I’m doing almost everything with AI during my internship. What do I do?” The work was getting done. They just couldn't have done it without the AI.

Dum's answer is to let AI handle the parts you already understand and make you learn the rest. Its skill tree starts empty. It won't write code for a skill until you've explained it in your own words or written it yourself.

Where the idea came from

I read a post on r/chess from a guy who had been stuck around 1800 for years. Over a few months he got to 2000, and he wasn't playing much. Most of that time went into teaching a friend who was rated around 400.

When you teach a beginner, you have to put into words the things you'd been doing by feel. Dum is built around that. You're the teacher, and Dum is the beginner.

What the research says

Learning by teaching has a name: the protégé effect. In two studies at Stanford, students put more effort into learning when they were teaching a computer character than when they were learning for themselves (Chase et al., 2009).

Anthropic ran a trial with 52 mostly junior developers learning a Python library they hadn't used before. Afterwards, the ones who had an AI assistant scored 50% on a quiz about it. The ones who coded by hand scored 67%. The biggest gap was on debugging, and the AI group wasn't significantly faster. The AI users who asked follow-up questions and requested explanations scored 65% or higher (Shen and Tamkin, 2026).

In a field experiment with nearly a thousand high school math students, GPT-4 raised grades while students had it. Once it was taken away, students who'd used a plain ChatGPT-style version did 17% worse than students who never had it. A version with prompts designed to protect learning mostly avoided that drop (Bastani et al., 2025).

So Dum takes on the work you already know how to do, and asks you questions about the rest.
